The problem

Dust takes a third of the output and nobody sees it coming.

Soiling loss is measured after the fact, by the gap between what the plant should have produced and what it did.

32%of output lost after eight months without cleaning at one desert site, and more than half after six months at another
20%of output lost from a single sandstorm, in one afternoon
10 daysbetween cleanings at a large plant that holds losses under one percent, whether or not the dust came

A solar plant in a dusty climate has two costs that fight each other. Leave the panels alone and the dust piles up, cutting output a little more every day and a lot more after a storm. Clean them and you spend water, labor, and vehicle hours, and you risk cleaning rows that were about to be cleaned by rain or that were barely dirty. Most operators resolve this with a calendar: every row, every ten days. The calendar does not know a storm is coming on day four.

Forecasting the dust is hard. Dust storms are local, fast, and poorly predicted by regional weather models; the best published models explain only about half the variation in dust events for inland cities. Soiling also varies across a plant: rows near a road or an open edge soil faster than rows in the interior. A plant wide number hides the rows that matter.

In plain terms

It is like washing a car on a schedule in a place where it might rain, or might get hit by a dust storm, and nobody can tell you which. Washing every Sunday wastes water the week it rains and leaves it filthy the week the storm comes. What you want is a forecast good enough to say wash Thursday, skip next week, and do the north side first.

Why the standard approach fails

A calendar cannot see a storm, and a weather model cannot see a row.

01

Fixed schedules clean the wrong rows on the wrong days

A ten day cycle cleans a row the day before a storm and leaves it dirty for nine days after. The schedule is optimized for the average week, which never happens.

What we do. The schedule is re planned every day from a dust forecast, and the plan is evolved against actual recovered energy, not against a rule of thumb.

02

Regional dust forecasts are too coarse

Public dust models work at tens of kilometers. A plant needs to know about its own site and, ideally, which side of it.

What we do. We fuse the regional forecast with the plant’s own sensors, satellite aerosol data, and the plant’s own soiling history to produce a site and row level forecast, and we evolve the fusion to the site.

03

Soiling models from one site do not transfer

Dust composition, humidity, wind, and panel tilt differ between sites. A model fit to one plant is wrong at the next.

What we do. Every plant gets its own evolved model, baselined on its own production history, with the portfolio’s other plants as a starting point rather than a template.

04

The objective is money, not cleanliness

Cleaning to a cleanliness target ignores the price of water, the cost of a crew, and the value of the energy on that day. A spotless panel on a cloudy day was not worth cleaning.

What we do. The schedule is optimized on recovered revenue minus cleaning cost, under water and crew constraints, with the plant’s own tariff and costs.

How we solve it

Forecast the dust. Evolve the schedule.

Two models, both evolved: one that predicts how dirty each row will be, and one that decides what to do about it.

In plain terms

We breed two things. First, a forecaster: dozens of versions that predict tomorrow’s dust on each row, tested against what actually landed, with the best ones kept and combined. Second, a schedule: dozens of candidate cleaning plans for the next two weeks, scored on how much energy they would recover against what they cost, with the best plans kept, mixed, and varied. The plant gets the plan that wins. The next day, both are updated with what actually happened.

  1. 01

    Connect what the plant already measures

    Inverter output per string, irradiance, weather station, any soiling sensors, cleaning logs, and the regional dust forecast. No new hardware is required to start; the plant’s own production history is the training signal, because the gap between expected and actual output is the soiling.

  2. 02

    Baseline the soiling, row by row

    We reconstruct how fast each row has been soiling over the past seasons, and how much each past cleaning recovered. This usually reveals that a handful of edge rows account for a disproportionate share of the loss.

  3. 03

    Evolve the dust forecaster

    A population of forecasting models is scored on predicted versus actual soiling per row, one to seven days ahead, with extra weight on storm days. The survivors are combined and varied. The objectives include accuracy on storm days, accuracy on edge rows, and calibration, so the schedule can trust the forecast’s confidence.

  4. 04

    Evolve the cleaning schedule

    A second population of candidate schedules, each a plan of which rows to clean on which days, is scored on recovered energy value minus water, labor, and vehicle cost, under the plant’s constraints: crew hours, water allocation, and no cleaning during high wind. Plans that violate a constraint are discarded. The best plan is issued; the crew follows it.

  5. 05

    Learn from every day of production

    Each day’s actual output updates the soiling estimate, the forecaster, and the schedule. Over a season the system learns the plant’s own dust: which wind brings it, which rows catch it, and when cleaning is worth the water.

What you get

More energy, less water, and a plan the crew can follow.

A row level dust forecast

One to seven days ahead, with confidence, updated daily.

A cleaning plan, not a calendar

Which rows, which day, in which order, under your crew and water limits.

Recovered energy you can audit

Every cleaning is scored on what it actually recovered, so the plan improves and the finance team can see it.

Storm response

When a storm is forecast, the plan shifts: hold cleaning before, prioritize after, by row.

Portfolio learning

Each plant has its own model. New plants start from the portfolio’s experience and evolve to their own site.

No new hardware to begin

Starts from inverter data and logs. Sensors improve it; they are not required to start.

Where this fits

Built for plants where the dust is the weather.

  • Utility scale solar in arid and dust prone regions
  • Owners and operators with cleaning crews, water budgets, and production targets
  • Portfolios where each plant soils differently and one policy fits none
  • Plants with soiling sensors that are read but not acted on
  • Any site where the gap between expected and actual output is large and unexplained

Soiling is the kind of problem that gets a rule of thumb because the forecast is hard and the loss is invisible on any single day. We take it on precisely because it is hard: the forecast has to be evolved to the site, the schedule has to be evolved to the costs, and the measure of success is a number on the revenue line.
