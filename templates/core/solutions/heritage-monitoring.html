The problem

Thousands of sites, one survey, and a clock that does not stop.

Heritage agencies are registering sites faster than anyone can monitor them.

A sandstone facade carved two thousand years ago is a structure with a decay rate. Daily heating and cooling open cracks. Wind carries sand that abrades. Rare rain finds the cracks and widens them. Tourism adds vibration and footfall. Geologists can assess a site and rank its risks, and they have. What they cannot do is come back every month to every site. The assessment is a snapshot; the decay is a film.

Repeat imagery is now cheap. A drone can fly a facade in an afternoon and a satellite passes over every few days. Comparing two images of the same cliff for change sounds simple. It is not: the sun moves, shadows move, sand drifts, cameras differ, and a one millimeter crack has to be found in a wall the size of a building. A general change detector flags the shadows. The one that matters flags the crack.

In plain terms

It is like comparing two photographs of your house taken a year apart to see if a crack has grown. If one was taken at noon and one at sunset, almost everything looks different and nothing is. What you need is a way to ignore the light and the dust and see the crack. Then you need to do it for fourteen thousand houses and send the inspector to the ones that changed.

Why the standard approach fails

Change detectors see the sun move. They need to see the rock move.

01

Light and shadow swamp the signal

On a carved facade, the difference between morning and afternoon is larger than the difference between this year and last. Generic change detection flags the time of day.

What we do. The detector is evolved per site on image pairs where the true change is known, so it learns to ignore that site’s light and keep that site’s cracks.

02

Every site is a different material

Sandstone, limestone, mud brick, and basalt weather differently and photograph differently. One model does not fit the register.

What we do. A model family evolved per material and per site, starting from the nearest neighbor in the portfolio rather than from scratch.

03

Real change is rare, so there is little to learn from

Most image pairs show no structural change. Models trained on that learn to say nothing.

What we do. Detection of every known past change event at the site is a hard floor, and the loop weights the rare true changes so the detector commits on them.

04

A flag is not a plan

A list of ten thousand possible changes is not useful to a team of five geologists.

What we do. Flags are ranked by the site’s existing risk assessment and the size of the change, into a survey schedule the team can actually fly.

How we solve it

Repeat imagery in. A ranked survey list out.

The same evolutionary loop as our medical imaging, pointed at a cliff instead of a lung.

In plain terms

We make dozens of versions of a program that compares two photographs of the same rock face and marks what changed. We test them on pairs where geologists already know the answer, including pairs taken at different times of day with nothing changed. Versions that flag shadows are thrown out. Versions that miss a known rockfall are thrown out. The survivors are combined and varied, and after a few rounds the program sees the cracks and not the sun. Then it runs on every new flight or satellite pass and tells the team where to go.

  1. 01

    Build the baseline from what exists

    Past drone surveys, satellite archives, the existing risk assessments, and any photographs with a date. Each site gets a reference model of its facades and slopes, and a record of what has already been documented as changed.

  2. 02

    Align repeat imagery

    New flights and satellite passes are registered to the reference so that the same pixel means the same square of rock across years, cameras, and seasons.

  3. 03

    Evolve the change detector per site

    Candidates are scored on known true changes caught, on false alarms from light and sand, on the minimum change size detected, and on consistency across seasons. Candidates that miss a documented past event are discarded. The conservation team chooses how sensitive the deployed detector should be.

  4. 04

    Rank and schedule

    Detected changes are ranked by the site’s risk assessment, the size and rate of the change, and visitor exposure, into a prioritized survey list. The team flies the top of the list. Every confirmed or dismissed flag feeds the next generation.

  5. 05

    Extend across the register

    Sites with no drone survey start with satellite imagery and a detector evolved from similar material elsewhere in the portfolio. As surveys accumulate, each site gets its own.

Finding a growing crack in a cliff the size of a building, across years of different light, is the same kind of problem as finding a small nodule in a chest scan across different scanners. It is the problem of a small true signal in a large, shifting background. We evolve for that signal, and we measure ourselves on the changes that were actually there.
