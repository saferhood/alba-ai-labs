The problem

The sensors see the crowd. Nobody sees the next two hours.

The largest gatherings on earth now take place in heat that exceeds what a human body can survive for long.

1,301deaths at one gathering in one year, most of them people outside the official system with no access to shade or transport
43 hoursover six days above the limit of human heat tolerance at that event, with the risk projected to rise several fold as the calendar moves into summer
26 hoursof transport delay recorded for one group in a prior year, in a system of twenty thousand buses and a rail line

Organizers of large events have invested heavily in seeing. Thousands of cameras, crowd density dashboards, thermal imaging, drones, wearable bracelets. What this produces is an accurate picture of the present: how many people are where, right now. It does not produce the thing medical teams need, which is a forecast. Which site will see a surge in heat collapse in two hours? Where should the next cooling station, water truck, and medical team be sent before the surge, not after?

Heat illness is predictable in aggregate. It follows temperature, humidity, sun exposure, crowd density, walking distance, time since the last water, and how many people at a site are outside the organized system. All of those are measured or measurable. What is missing is the model that combines them into a forecast and the loop that improves it, year after year, from what actually happened.

In plain terms

A weather forecast tells you it will be 48 degrees at two o’clock. A heat illness forecast tells you that at two o’clock, the unshaded walkway between sites four and five will produce thirty collapses an hour, and that the clinic at site five has capacity for ten. That is the difference between knowing it is hot and knowing where to send the ambulances.

Why the standard approach fails

Monitoring is not forecasting, and last year is not this year.

01

Dashboards show now, not next

Crowd analytics report density and movement in real time. By the time density at a site is alarming, the collapses are already arriving at the clinic.

What we do. We forecast two to six hours ahead, by site and by hour, so resources move before the surge.

02

The people at most risk are the least measured

Those outside the organized system have no bracelet, no bus seat, and no cooled tent. They walk the farthest and appear in the data only when they collapse.

What we do. Camera counts, not registrations, are the crowd signal, and the model is evolved to weight unregistered density as the risk it is.

03

Each year the layout, the calendar, and the heat change

A model fit to last year’s event is wrong about this year’s shade, this year’s routes, and this year’s weather.

What we do. The model is re evolved each season on the current layout and the forecast climate, and updated every hour during the event from live clinic arrivals.

04

Under forecasting at the peak is the failure that kills

A model tuned for average accuracy will be slightly low on the worst hour. The worst hour is the only one that matters.

What we do. Under forecasting at peak hours is a hard constraint. A candidate that misses the peak is discarded regardless of its average.

How we solve it

A forecast on top of the sensors you already have.

We do not replace the cameras. We give them a future tense.

In plain terms

We make dozens of versions of a model that predicts how many people will need help at each site, each hour. We test them against past years, hour by hour, and throw out any version that under predicted a peak. The survivors are combined and varied, and the next generation is tested again. During the event, the model sees live clinic arrivals every hour and adjusts. Medical and logistics teams get a map of the next six hours, not a replay of the last one.

  1. 01

    Fuse the signals that exist

    Hourly weather and sun angle, a shade map of each site and route, crowd density from the camera network, transport loading, water point status, and clinic arrivals by site. Wearable data is used where it exists and is never required.

  2. 02

    Baseline on past events

    We reconstruct previous events hour by hour: where the collapses happened, how far people had walked, how hot and how crowded it was. This is the ground truth the forecaster is evolved against.

  3. 03

    Evolve the forecaster with a peak floor

    Candidates are scored on forecast error by site and hour, on calibration, and on lead time. Any candidate that under forecast a peak hour in a past event is discarded. The surviving set is handed to the medical command, who choose how conservative the deployed forecast should be.

  4. 04

    Evolve the response plan

    Given the forecast, a second population of plans decides where cooling stations, water, medics, and shuttle capacity go, hour by hour, under the real constraints of how fast a team can move through a crowd. Plans are scored on forecast cases covered within clinic capacity.

  5. 05

    Update every hour, and every year

    During the event, live clinic arrivals correct the forecast hourly. After the event, the full record becomes training data for next year’s generation, with the new layout and calendar.
